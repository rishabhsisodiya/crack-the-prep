## 1. Requirements

**Functional**
- Browse movies/events by city, venue and date; see showtimes.
- View a live **seat map** for a show and pick specific seats.
- **Hold** the selected seats while the user pays (typically 5–10 minutes), then confirm or release them.
- Cancel a booking (refund rules are a business policy, out of scope).

**Non-functional**
- **A seat is sold at most once.** Same invariant as hotels, but the unit is a *specific seat*, not a count.
- **Spiky, extreme contention** — a big release or a concert on-sale puts hundreds of thousands of users on the same few thousand seats within minutes.
- Browsing can be eventually consistent; holding and confirming must be strongly consistent.
- Fair-ish ordering during on-sales; the system must stay up even when demand exceeds capacity by orders of magnitude.

**Estimates.** A national platform: ~50k shows/day, ~200 seats/show average → ~10M seat-nights/day of inventory. Normal booking rate is modest (hundreds/s). The design driver is the on-sale peak: 1M users arriving in 10 minutes for one 50k-seat stadium.

- **Catalog** (movies, venues, showtimes) is read-heavy and nearly static — serve from CDN/cache.
- **Seat map** is a per-show read of seat statuses. Serve it from a cache updated by inventory change events; it's allowed to be a second or two stale because the hold step re-checks.
- **Booking service** owns the only writes: hold, confirm, release. All seat state changes go through it.

## 3. Seat inventory model — the key design decision

Unlike hotels (sell a room *type*, count is enough), cinemas and stadiums sell **specific seats** — the user picks F12. So inventory is one row per seat per show:

`show_seats(show_id, seat_id, status, hold_id, hold_expires_at, booking_id, version)` with `status ∈ {AVAILABLE, HELD, BOOKED}`.

A 200-seat screen is 200 rows per show — small. A stadium is ~50k rows for one event, still fine for one partition.

**Holding seats atomically.** The user selects 4 seats; either all 4 become held or none do:

```sql
UPDATE show_seats
SET status = 'HELD', hold_id = :h, hold_expires_at = now() + interval '8 minutes'
WHERE show_id = :s AND seat_id IN (:seats)
  AND (status = 'AVAILABLE' OR (status = 'HELD' AND hold_expires_at < now()));
-- rows affected must equal number of seats requested; otherwise ROLLBACK
```

The `hold_expires_at < now()` clause lets an expired hold be taken over immediately, so correctness doesn't depend on the sweeper running on time. The sweeper only tidies up statuses for the seat map.

**Confirming** after payment: `UPDATE ... SET status='BOOKED', booking_id=:b WHERE hold_id=:h AND status='HELD' AND hold_expires_at > now()`. If this matches zero rows, the hold expired while the user was paying — **refund automatically**, don't book.

<Callout type="tip">
Name the payment race explicitly: payment succeeds at minute 8:01 but the hold expired at 8:00 and someone else grabbed the seat. The fix is (a) a confirm that is conditional on the hold still being valid, (b) automatic refund when it isn't, and (c) a small grace period — extend the hold once when the user is redirected to the payment page, so the race is rare rather than common.
</Callout>

## 4. Deep dive — surviving the on-sale spike

A Taylor-Swift-scale on-sale is the real interview question. The database isn't the first thing to fail; everything in front of it is.

1. **Virtual waiting room.** Before the sale opens, users land in a queue page served from the CDN. At open, they're assigned a random position (randomizing at open is fairer than first-come, which rewards bots and people with fast connections). The waiting room admits users at a fixed rate the backend can sustain and hands each a **signed, short-lived admission token**; the API gateway rejects booking calls without one.
2. **Shard the hot event in memory.** For one hot show, keep seat state in Redis (a bitmap or hash per show/section) and perform holds with a Lua script — check all requested seats are free and mark them held in one atomic step. Persist to the DB asynchronously via a log; the DB stays the system of record for confirmed bookings.
3. **Split by section.** A stadium's sections are independent; partition hold traffic by `(show_id, section)` so one hot section doesn't serialize the whole venue.
4. **Seat map polling, not per-seat push.** Pushing every seat change to 1M clients is a fan-out storm. Clients poll a cached seat map every few seconds, or get a coarse "section availability" view, and only fetch a section's seats when they zoom in.
5. **Best-available instead of pick-a-seat** under extreme load: the server allocates seats from a pre-computed ordering, which removes the "two users fight over F12" conflict entirely.

<Callout type="warn">
Bots are a first-class requirement for big on-sales, not an afterthought: per-account purchase limits, verified-fan presale codes, CAPTCHA at queue entry, and token binding (admission token tied to the session) are standard. Mention them; interviewers at ticketing companies will ask.
</Callout>

## 5. What real systems do today

- **Ticketmaster** runs a queue/waiting-room system for big on-sales and the "Verified Fan" presale, which pre-registers fans and distributes access codes to reduce bot demand. The 2022 Eras Tour presale is the standard example of demand (reportedly billions of system requests) overwhelming even a queued system.
- **Queue-it** and **Cloudflare Waiting Room** are off-the-shelf virtual waiting rooms used by ticketing and retail sites; both work by admitting users at a configured rate and holding the rest on a cheap static page.
- Cinema chains and aggregators (BookMyShow-style) commonly hold seats for a fixed window (around 5–10 minutes) during payment, with the hold timer visible to the user.

## 6. Scaling & failure

- **Seat inventory DB primary dies** → stop accepting holds (fail closed). Browsing continues from cache. Never accept a hold you can't write durably.
- **Payment gateway slow** → holds expire while users wait. Extend holds on redirect to payment, and reconcile with the gateway's webhooks; a payment that succeeds after expiry triggers an automatic refund.
- **Redis (hot-event seat state) dies** → rebuild from the DB plus the hold log, admit nobody from the waiting room until it's back. This is why holds are logged before acknowledgement.
- **Abandoned holds** inflate "sold out" — keep TTLs short and let expired holds be taken over directly in the hold `WHERE` clause.

## Interview follow-ups

- *"How is this different from hotel booking?"* — Hotels sell interchangeable units (a count per room type per night); cinemas sell specific seats, so inventory is per-seat rows and the conflict is on individual seats, not a counter.
- *"User selects 4 seats, one is taken — what happens?"* — The multi-seat hold is atomic; rows-affected ≠ 4 rolls back and the user re-picks. No partial holds.
- *"Payment succeeds after the hold expired."* — Conditional confirm fails, refund automatically, apologize; reduce frequency with a hold extension on payment redirect.
- *"1M users hit a 50k-seat on-sale at 10:00."* — Waiting room with randomized positions, rate-based admission tokens, in-memory hold processing per section, best-available allocation, bot controls.
- *"Why not just put a distributed lock around each seat?"* — Locks plus expiries still race; the atomic conditional update (or Lua script) on the seat state is the real guarantee.
- *"How do you show a live seat map to a million viewers?"* — Cached snapshot with short polling and section-level aggregation, not per-seat WebSocket pushes.
